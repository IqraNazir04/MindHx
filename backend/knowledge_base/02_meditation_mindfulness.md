## What mindfulness and meditation are

Mindfulness means paying attention to the present moment, on purpose, without judging it. Meditation is the practice used to train that attention, for example by focusing on the breath, body sensations, sounds, or phrases of kindness. Mindfulness is not about emptying the mind or forcing calm; noticing that the mind wandered and gently bringing it back *is* the practice.

Evidence: structured programmes such as Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction (MBSR) and Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy (MBCT) show moderate benefits for anxiety, depression, stress and chronic pain. NICE recommends MBCT to help prevent relapse in people with recurrent depression who are currently well. Meditation is a helpful complement to care, not a replacement for treatment of moderate or severe conditions.

## Cautions before practising

Most people find meditation safe, but some experience increased anxiety, distressing memories, or feeling disconnected (dissociation), especially with long silent sessions. Extra care is needed for people with a history of trauma or PTSD, active psychosis, severe depression, or bipolar disorder. Helpful adjustments: keep eyes open, use shorter sessions (3–5 minutes), anchor to sounds or the feet rather than the breath, and stop at any time. If practice consistently worsens distress, the person should stop and speak with a mental health professional.

## Technique: Slow exhale breathing (2 minutes)

Best for: quick calm during stress or before sleep.
1. Sit or lie comfortably. Let your shoulders drop.
2. Breathe in gently through your nose for a count of 4.
3. Breathe out slowly through your mouth or nose for a count of 6, as if blowing through a straw.
4. Repeat for 8 to 10 breaths.
A longer out-breath than in-breath signals the nervous system to slow down. If counting feels stressful, just make each out-breath a little longer and softer than the in-breath. Stop if you feel dizzy.

<!-- chunk: id=med_004 | domain=meditation | type=technique | risk=low | audience=general | lang=en -->
## Technique: Box breathing

Best for: focus under pressure.
Breathe in for 4, hold for 4, breathe out for 4, hold for 4. Picture tracing the sides of a square. Repeat 4 to 6 rounds. People with breathing conditions, heart problems, or panic attacks triggered by breath-holding can skip the holds and simply breathe in 4, out 4.

<!-- chunk: id=med_005 | domain=meditation | type=technique | risk=low | audience=general | lang=en -->
## Technique: Physiological sigh

Best for: rapid relief from acute stress.
Take a normal breath in through the nose, then a short second sip of air on top of it, then a long, slow breath out through the mouth. Repeat 2 to 5 times. A 2023 Stanford study found daily cyclic sighing improved mood and lowered breathing rate more than mindfulness meditation in that sample, though the study was small.

## Technique: 5-4-3-2-1 grounding

Best for: panic, overwhelming emotions, feeling detached.
Notice and name, silently or aloud:
- 5 things you can see
- 4 things you can feel (feet on the floor, clothes on skin, the chair)
- 3 things you can hear
- 2 things you can smell
- 1 thing you can taste, or one slow breath
This moves attention from inner alarm to the present surroundings. Saying things aloud and describing them in detail ("a blue cup with a chip on the handle") makes it more effective.

## Technique: Body scan (10 minutes)

Best for: tension, sleep, reconnecting with the body.
1. Lie down or sit back. Close your eyes or soften your gaze.
2. Bring attention to your toes. Notice any sensation: warmth, pressure, tingling, or nothing at all. All are fine.
3. Slowly move attention up: feet, ankles, calves, knees, thighs, hips, belly, chest, back, hands, arms, shoulders, neck, jaw, face, scalp.
4. Where you find tension, imagine breathing into that area and letting it soften on the out-breath. You don't need to force it to relax.
5. When the mind wanders, notice where it went and return to the body part you were on.
6. Finish by sensing the whole body at once for a few breaths.
For people with trauma or chronic pain, skip any area that feels distressing and rest attention on a neutral spot such as the hands.

<!-- chunk: id=med_008 | domain=meditation | type=technique | risk=low | audience=general | lang=en -->
## Technique: Progressive muscle relaxation (PMR)

Best for: physical tension, anxiety, insomnia.
Working through muscle groups (hands, arms, shoulders, face, stomach, legs, feet), tense each group gently for about 5 seconds, then release for 10 to 15 seconds, noticing the difference between tension and relaxation. Tense to about half strength, never to the point of pain. Skip any injured area. Practising daily for two weeks makes relaxation easier to call on during stress.

<!-- chunk: id=med_009 | domain=meditation | type=technique | risk=low | audience=general | lang=en -->
## Technique: Loving-kindness (metta) meditation

Best for: self-criticism, loneliness, anger, grief.
Sit comfortably and silently repeat phrases, first for yourself, then for someone you love, a neutral person, someone difficult, and finally all people:
"May I be safe. May I be healthy. May I be at peace. May I be kind to myself."
If directing kindness at yourself feels hard, start with someone you love easily and return to yourself later. Studies link loving-kindness practice to more positive emotions and less self-criticism.

<!-- chunk: id=med_010 | domain=meditation | type=technique | risk=low | audience=general | lang=en -->
## Technique: Mindful walking and everyday mindfulness

Walking slowly and noticing each step, the lift, movement and placement of the foot, is a good option for people who find sitting still difficult. Everyday activities can also be practice: drinking tea while noticing warmth, smell and taste; washing hands while feeling the water. For many Muslim users, wudu and salah can naturally be practised with this kind of present-moment attention; MindHx can mention this when the user brings up faith, without assuming religious practice.

<!-- chunk: id=med_011 | domain=meditation | type=technique | risk=low | audience=general | lang=en -->
## Technique: STOP practice (1 minute)

For busy moments: **S**top what you're doing. **T**ake a breath. **O**bserve what is happening in your body, emotions and thoughts. **P**roceed with something helpful. This short pause interrupts automatic reactions.

## Building a practice

Start small: 3 to 5 minutes daily is more useful than 30 minutes once a week. Attach practice to an existing habit (after Fajr, after brushing teeth, before sleep). Expect a busy mind; it is not failure. Guided audio helps beginners. Benefits typically build over 4 to 8 weeks of regular practice.
