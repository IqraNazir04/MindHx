## What stress is

Stress is the body and mind's response to demands that feel challenging or threatening. The World Health Organization describes it as a natural human reaction that prompts us to address challenges, and everyone experiences it to some degree. Short-term (acute) stress can be useful: it sharpens attention and gives energy before an exam, a deadline or an emergency. Problems begin when stress is intense, frequent or long-lasting (chronic) and the person has little chance to recover between demands.

Stress is not a mental illness on its own, but chronic stress raises the risk of anxiety disorders, depression, sleep problems, high blood pressure, heart disease, digestive problems and weakened immunity.

## What happens in the body during stress

When the brain perceives threat, the amygdala signals the hypothalamus, which activates two systems. The sympathetic nervous system releases adrenaline within seconds, raising heart rate, breathing and muscle tension (the "fight, flight or freeze" response). The HPA axis (hypothalamus–pituitary–adrenal) releases cortisol over minutes to hours, raising blood sugar and keeping the body on alert. Once the threat passes, the parasympathetic nervous system ("rest and digest") should bring the body back to baseline.

In chronic stress the alarm doesn't fully switch off. Cortisol stays elevated, sleep becomes lighter, digestion and immunity suffer, and the brain becomes more sensitive to threat. This is why slow breathing, exercise, sleep and social connection help: they activate the recovery system directly.

## Common signs of stress

- **Emotional:** irritability, feeling overwhelmed, worry, low mood, tearfulness, feeling on edge.
- **Cognitive:** racing thoughts, difficulty concentrating, forgetfulness, indecision, constant "what if" thinking.
- **Physical:** headaches, tight shoulders or jaw, stomach upset, fatigue, rapid heartbeat, sweating, changes in appetite, poor sleep.
- **Behavioural:** withdrawing from people, procrastinating, overworking, increased smoking, caffeine, or reliance on screens or substances.

## Burnout

Burnout is listed in ICD-11 as an occupational phenomenon, not a medical condition. It results from chronic workplace stress that has not been successfully managed and has three features: exhaustion or energy depletion; increased mental distance from one's job, or feelings of negativity or cynicism about it; and reduced professional effectiveness. Caregivers, healthcare workers, teachers and people in high-demand jobs are at particular risk.

Burnout and depression overlap. If low mood, loss of pleasure, or hopelessness spread beyond work into the rest of life, screening for depression (PHQ-9) is recommended.

## Evidence-based ways to manage stress

1. **Name it.** Labelling the feeling ("I'm stressed about my deadline") reduces its intensity.
2. **Slow breathing.** Breathing out longer than you breathe in activates the parasympathetic system (see meditation file for scripts).
3. **Move your body.** Around 150 minutes a week of moderate activity is linked to lower anxiety and better mood; even a 10-minute walk helps.
4. **Protect sleep.** Regular sleep and wake times matter more than total hours at first.
5. **Problem-solve what's solvable.** Write the problem down, list options, pick one small step, act, review.
6. **Accept what isn't.** For things outside your control, practise letting go of the struggle rather than the feeling.
7. **Connect.** Talking with someone trusted is one of the strongest buffers against stress.
8. **Limit stimulants and alcohol.** Caffeine late in the day and alcohol both worsen sleep and anxiety.
9. **Set boundaries.** Saying no, and scheduling recovery time, are skills, not selfishness.
10. **Use the WHO "Doing What Matters in Times of Stress" approach:** grounding, unhooking from difficult thoughts, acting on your values, being kind to yourself, and making room for difficult feelings.

## The stress bucket (explaining stress simply)

Imagine a bucket. Stressors (work, money, family, health) pour water in. How big the bucket is depends on things like genetics, past experiences and current support. Coping strategies act as a tap that lets water out. Helpful taps include exercise, talking, rest and problem-solving. Unhelpful taps (avoidance, alcohol, overworking) may release a little water but often refill the bucket later. When the bucket overflows, people notice symptoms. The goal is to reduce inflow where possible and open helpful taps regularly, not only in emergencies.

## When stress needs professional help

Encourage a professional consultation when stress:
- lasts more than two weeks and interferes with work, study, relationships or self-care
- comes with persistent low mood, loss of interest, or hopelessness
- causes panic attacks, inability to sleep most nights, or physical symptoms with no clear cause
- leads to heavy reliance on alcohol, drugs, or medications
- comes with any thoughts of self-harm (follow the safety protocol)
