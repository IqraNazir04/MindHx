## Acute and chronic pain

**Acute pain** is a short-term warning signal linked to injury or illness and usually settles as the body heals. **Chronic pain** lasts or recurs for more than 3 months. ICD-11 separates *chronic primary pain* (pain that is a condition in itself, such as fibromyalgia, chronic primary low back pain, or many chronic headaches) from *chronic secondary pain* (pain caused by another condition, such as arthritis, cancer, or nerve damage). Around one in five adults worldwide lives with chronic pain, and it is closely linked with depression, anxiety and poor sleep in both directions.

## Pain is real, and the brain is involved

All pain is produced by the brain as a protective response. It is real, whether or not a scan shows damage. The **biopsychosocial model** explains that pain is shaped by biology (tissue, nerves, inflammation), psychology (attention, fear, mood, beliefs, past experience), and social context (work, family, support, culture).

**Gate control theory** (Melzack and Wall, 1965) proposed that signals from the spinal cord can be turned up or down before reaching the brain. Stress, fear and focusing on pain open the "gate"; relaxation, distraction, movement and positive emotions help close it. In long-term pain the nervous system can become oversensitive (**central sensitisation**), so pain persists or spreads even after tissues heal. This is why psychological approaches can genuinely reduce pain, not because pain is "in your head".

## The fear–avoidance cycle

After pain, some people begin to fear movement and avoid activity. Avoidance leads to deconditioning, stiffness, low mood, and isolation, which amplify pain and fear further. **Pain catastrophising** (constantly thinking about pain, magnifying it, and feeling helpless) predicts worse pain and disability. Breaking the cycle involves understanding pain, gradual return to activity, and changing the relationship with pain rather than waiting for it to vanish first.

## Evidence-based approaches to chronic pain

NICE guideline NG193 (2021) on chronic primary pain recommends:
- **Exercise and physical activity programmes**, tailored and gradual.
- **Psychological therapy**: Cognitive Behavioural Therapy (CBT) for pain and Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT).
- **Acupuncture** in certain settings.
- Some antidepressants may be considered by a doctor, even when the person isn't depressed.

NICE advises against *starting* opioids, NSAIDs, paracetamol, benzodiazepines or gabapentinoids for chronic primary pain because evidence of benefit is weak and harms are real. Any changes to medication must be made with a doctor; MindHx should never advise stopping or adjusting pain medication.

Other helpful strategies with good evidence: pain education ("explain pain"), mindfulness-based programmes, good sleep, and multidisciplinary pain management programmes.

## Self-management skills for persistent pain

1. **Pacing.** Find a baseline amount of activity you can do on good and bad days, then increase it slowly (around 10% at a time). This avoids the "boom and bust" pattern of overdoing it on good days and crashing afterwards.
2. **Graded activity.** Gently reintroduce valued activities in small, planned steps.
3. **Relaxation and breathing.** Slow exhale breathing and progressive muscle relaxation (gentle, no straining) reduce muscle guarding.
4. **Attention shifting.** Absorbing activities, conversation and music lower pain intensity.
5. **Thought work.** Notice catastrophic thoughts ("this will never end", "I can't cope") and try more balanced ones ("this is a bad flare, flares pass, I have ways to get through it").
6. **Flare-up plan.** Write down what helps during flares in advance.
7. **Sleep routine.** Poor sleep lowers pain tolerance the next day.
8. **Values-based living (ACT).** Ask "what matters to me, and what small step toward it can I take today, with the pain coming along?"

## Pain, mood and emotional pain

Chronic pain roughly doubles the risk of depression, and depression lowers pain thresholds. People living with chronic pain also have a higher risk of suicidal thoughts. MindHx should gently ask about mood and hopelessness when users describe long-term pain, and follow the safety protocol if needed. Emotional pain (heartbreak, rejection, loss) activates overlapping brain regions with physical pain, which is why "hurt" is not just a metaphor.

## Red flags: pain that needs urgent medical attention

MindHx must not attribute these to stress. Advise urgent medical care or emergency services (1122 / 115 in Pakistan) for:
- chest pain, pressure, or pain spreading to arm, jaw or back, especially with breathlessness or sweating
- sudden, severe "worst ever" headache, or headache with fever, stiff neck, confusion, weakness, or vision loss
- new back pain with numbness around the groin, loss of bladder or bowel control, or leg weakness
- severe abdominal pain, especially with vomiting blood, black stools, or a rigid abdomen
- pain after significant injury, or with fever, unexplained weight loss, or a history of cancer
- one-sided weakness, facial droop, or slurred speech (possible stroke)
