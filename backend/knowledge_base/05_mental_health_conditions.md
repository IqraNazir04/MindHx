## Generalised Anxiety Disorder (GAD)

GAD involves excessive, hard-to-control worry about multiple areas of life (health, work, family, finances) on most days for at least 6 months, along with restlessness, fatigue, difficulty concentrating, irritability, muscle tension, and sleep disturbance (ICD-11 6B00, DSM-5-TR). The GAD-7 screening tool scores 0–21; 5–9 mild, 10–14 moderate, 15+ severe. First-line treatments: CBT and, for moderate-severe cases, SSRIs/SNRIs prescribed by a doctor. MindHx can offer psychoeducation and grounding techniques but should not diagnose.

## Panic disorder and panic attacks

A panic attack is a sudden surge of intense fear peaking within minutes, with symptoms such as racing heart, chest tightness, shortness of breath, dizziness, trembling, sweating, nausea, and fear of dying or losing control. Attacks are frightening but not dangerous; they pass. Panic disorder is diagnosed when attacks are recurrent and followed by at least a month of worry about further attacks or avoidance of situations. Hyperventilation often drives the physical symptoms, so slow exhale breathing (see meditation file) and staying in place rather than fleeing help break the cycle. CBT with interoceptive exposure is the leading evidence-based treatment.

## Major depressive disorder

Depression involves low mood or loss of interest/pleasure most of the day, nearly every day, for at least 2 weeks, plus symptoms such as sleep or appetite change, fatigue, worthlessness or guilt, concentration difficulty, psychomotor changes, or thoughts of death (ICD-11 6A70, DSM-5-TR). Severity is often screened with PHQ-9 (0–27): 5–9 mild, 10–14 moderate, 15–19 moderately severe, 20+ severe. Any score above 0 on item 9 (thoughts of self-harm or being better off dead) triggers the safety protocol regardless of total score. NICE NG222 recommends a stepped approach: guided self-help and structured exercise for mild depression; CBT, interpersonal therapy, or antidepressants for moderate; combined therapy and medication for severe.

## Obsessive-Compulsive Disorder (OCD)

OCD involves obsessions (intrusive, unwanted thoughts, images or urges that cause distress, such as fears of contamination, harm, or taboo thoughts) and compulsions (repetitive behaviours or mental acts performed to reduce the distress or prevent a feared outcome, such as washing, checking, counting, or silent praying in a ritualised way). People with OCD usually know the thoughts are excessive but feel unable to stop the cycle. Intrusive thoughts themselves (including disturbing or violent ones) are extremely common in the general population; what distinguishes OCD is the distress and the compulsive response, not the content of the thought. First-line treatment is Exposure and Response Prevention (ERP), a form of CBT, with SSRIs (often at higher doses than for depression) as an adjunct or alternative.

## Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder (PTSD)

PTSD can follow exposure to actual or threatened death, serious injury, sexual violence, or witnessing such events. Core features: intrusive re-experiencing (flashbacks, nightmares), avoidance of reminders, persistent heightened threat perception (hypervigilance, exaggerated startle), and symptoms lasting more than a month causing significant impairment. ICD-11 separates PTSD from **Complex PTSD**, which adds persistent problems in emotion regulation, self-concept (shame, feeling diminished), and relationships, usually following prolonged or repeated trauma. First-line treatments: trauma-focused CBT, EMDR (Eye Movement Desensitisation and Reprocessing), and in some cases medication. MindHx should never ask a user to recount traumatic details for processing; that work belongs with a trained trauma therapist.

## Bipolar disorder

Bipolar disorder involves episodes of mania or hypomania (elevated or irritable mood, decreased need for sleep, grandiosity, rapid speech, impulsivity, risky behaviour) alongside episodes of depression. Mania (Bipolar I) typically causes marked impairment or requires hospitalisation; hypomania (Bipolar II) is less severe. Untreated mania can involve poor judgment with serious financial, legal, or safety consequences, and switches to depression carry suicide risk. Treatment is primarily medical (mood stabilisers, sometimes antipsychotics) combined with psychoeducation and therapy; MindHx should encourage psychiatric assessment rather than attempting to manage mood episodes through conversation.

## Psychosis and early warning signs

Psychosis involves a loss of the usual connection with shared reality: hallucinations (hearing, seeing, or feeling things others don't), delusions (fixed false beliefs not shared by one's culture, e.g. being watched or controlled), and disorganised thinking or speech. Early signs can be subtle: social withdrawal, declining performance at work or school, unusual suspiciousness, or vague odd beliefs, before clear hallucinations or delusions appear. Early intervention improves outcomes substantially. If a user describes experiences suggesting psychosis, MindHx should respond non-judgementally, avoid arguing about whether the experience is "real," and encourage prompt assessment by a psychiatrist, especially if there is any risk to the person or others.

## Insomnia and sleep problems

Chronic insomnia involves difficulty falling or staying asleep, or non-restorative sleep, at least 3 nights a week for 3 months or more, with daytime impact. It commonly co-occurs with anxiety and depression in both directions. First-line treatment is CBT for Insomnia (CBT-I): consistent sleep and wake times, getting out of bed when unable to sleep rather than lying awake frustrated, limiting daytime naps, reducing caffeine and screens before bed, and using the bed only for sleep (and intimacy). Sleeping medication is generally short-term only, due to dependence risk, and should be managed by a doctor.

## Postpartum depression and anxiety

Up to 1 in 5 mothers experience postpartum depression or anxiety, typically within the first year after birth; fathers and partners can also be affected. This differs from the much more common and short-lived "baby blues" (tearfulness, mood swings in the first 2 weeks). Warning signs include persistent sadness, excessive guilt about parenting, loss of interest in the baby or in previously enjoyed activities, intrusive frightening thoughts about the baby, and in rare, severe cases (postpartum psychosis), confusion, hallucinations, or thoughts of harming the baby — the latter is a psychiatric emergency requiring immediate care. Postpartum depression is treatable and is not a sign of being a bad parent; stigma and family pressure in Pakistan can make disclosure harder, so MindHx should respond with extra gentleness and normalise seeking help.

## How MindHx should use this information

MindHx is not a diagnostic tool. Screening questionnaires (GAD-7, PHQ-9) can be offered to help a person describe their experience and track change over time, but results should always be framed as a starting point for conversation with a professional, never as a diagnosis. Language should stay descriptive ("this sounds consistent with what's often called...") rather than definitive ("you have..."). Any mention of self-harm, suicide, mania, psychosis, or risk to a baby or another person should route through the safety protocol.
