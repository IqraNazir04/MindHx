## What grief is

Grief is the natural response to loss. It most often follows the death of someone close, but people also grieve miscarriage, divorce, the loss of health, a job, a home, a country, or a future they had imagined. Grief is not an illness; it reflects the bond that existed. It affects emotions (sadness, yearning, anger, guilt, relief, numbness), thoughts (preoccupation with the person, disbelief, difficulty concentrating), the body (fatigue, sleep and appetite changes, aches, tightness in the chest), and behaviour (crying, withdrawing, searching, keeping busy).

## The "five stages" and what research actually says

The well-known stages (denial, anger, bargaining, depression, acceptance) came from Elisabeth Kübler-Ross's work with dying patients, not bereaved people. Research does not support grief moving through fixed stages in order. People move back and forth between feelings, sometimes within the same hour. The stages can still be useful words for feelings people recognise, as long as no one is told they are grieving "wrong".

More current models:
- **Dual Process Model (Stroebe & Schut):** healthy grieving oscillates between *loss-oriented* coping (crying, remembering, yearning) and *restoration-oriented* coping (handling new tasks, new roles, taking breaks from grief). Both are needed; time away from grief is not avoidance.
- **Continuing bonds:** maintaining a connection with the person who died (talking to them, keeping traditions, prayer, charity in their name) is normal and often helpful, rather than a sign of failing to "let go".
- **Growing around grief (Tonkin):** grief may not shrink much, but life gradually grows around it.

## Normal grief vs prolonged grief disorder

Most bereaved people experience gradual easing over months, with waves of grief around anniversaries, Eid, and family events. A minority, roughly 7–10% of bereaved adults, develop **Prolonged Grief Disorder (PGD)**.

- **ICD-11:** persistent, pervasive longing for or preoccupation with the deceased, with intense emotional pain (sadness, guilt, anger, denial, difficulty accepting the death, feeling part of oneself was lost, inability to feel positive mood, emotional numbness, difficulty engaging in activities) lasting at least **6 months** after the loss and exceeding cultural and religious norms, causing significant impairment.
- **DSM-5-TR:** at least **12 months** after the death in adults (6 months in children and adolescents), with intense yearning or preoccupation nearly every day plus at least three of: identity disruption, marked disbelief, avoidance of reminders, intense emotional pain, difficulty reintegrating, emotional numbness, feeling life is meaningless, intense loneliness.

Grief and depression can co-exist. Features pointing to depression rather than grief alone: pervasive low mood not focused on the loss, persistent worthlessness, and loss of pleasure across all of life. MindHx should never diagnose PGD; it should recognise when grief seems stuck and suggest professional assessment.

## Complicating factors

Grief tends to be harder after sudden or violent deaths, deaths by suicide, the death of a child, losses with unresolved conflict, losses that others don't recognise (miscarriage, a partner the family didn't accept, a pet), multiple losses close together, or when the bereaved person was a long-term caregiver. People bereaved by suicide face higher risk themselves and benefit from early, specialised support.

## Coping with grief: what helps

- Let feelings come and go; there is no deadline.
- Keep basic routines: meals, water, sleep, short walks.
- Talk about the person and use their name. Share stories.
- Allow breaks from grief without guilt (Dual Process Model).
- Find ways to keep a bond: write a letter to them, keep a memory box, continue something they valued, give sadqa/charity or pray in their name if that fits the person's faith.
- Prepare for hard days such as anniversaries, Eid, birthdays; plan who you'll be with.
- Postpone major decisions (selling a house, moving) for several months where possible.
- Accept practical help. People often want to help but don't know how.
- Notice self-blame. Guilt ("I should have...") is very common and is usually hindsight, not reality.

## Supporting someone who is grieving

Show up and keep showing up, especially after the first few weeks when others drift away. Offer specific help ("I'll bring dinner on Thursday") rather than "let me know if you need anything". Say the person's name. Listen without trying to fix. Avoid phrases that minimise, such as "they're in a better place", "everything happens for a reason", or "at least...", unless the bereaved person uses that language themselves. "I'm so sorry. I'm here." is often enough.

## Grief in children

Children grieve in bursts: deeply sad one moment, playing the next. Use clear words ("died", not "went to sleep" or "we lost him"), which can frighten or confuse. Keep routines, answer questions honestly at their level, allow them to attend rituals if they want, and reassure them they are cared for and didn't cause the death. Watch for prolonged changes in sleep, school, regression, or withdrawal, and seek a child mental health professional if these persist.

<!-- chunk: id=grief_008 | domain=grief | type=treatment | risk=moderate | audience=general | lang=en -->
## Professional support for grief

Most people don't need therapy for grief, and routine grief counselling for everyone hasn't shown clear benefit. Targeted help is effective for those struggling: **Prolonged Grief Disorder Therapy** (Shear), grief-focused CBT, and support groups. Seek help when grief stays intense and disabling beyond 6 to 12 months, when there are thoughts of wanting to die to be with the person, when daily functioning has collapsed, or when alcohol or drugs are being used to cope.

## Cultural notes for Pakistan

In Pakistan, grief is often shared publicly through janaza, soyem/qul, chaliswan and visits of condolence, which can provide strong community support. After the formal mourning period ends, the bereaved may feel pressure to "be strong" or return to normal quickly, especially men, and widows may face practical and social hardships. MindHx should respect religious and cultural practices the user describes, avoid imposing any belief, and recognise that faith can be a source of comfort for some and a source of questioning for others; both are normal.
