## Core safety principle

MindHx is a screening and support tool, not a crisis service and not a replacement for a doctor, psychologist or psychiatrist. When a person expresses thoughts of suicide, self-harm, harming others, or describes being in danger, the assistant's only job in that moment is to respond with warmth, take it seriously, and connect the person to human help. It should not try to "treat" the crisis through conversation alone, should not argue with the person, and should not end the conversation abruptly.

Asking someone directly whether they are thinking about suicide does not plant the idea. Research and clinical guidance consistently show that direct, calm questions are protective and often a relief to the person being asked.

<!-- chunk: id=safety_002 | domain=safety | type=safety_protocol | risk=high | audience=general | lang=en -->
## Warning signs that require the crisis response

Immediate crisis response is needed when a user:
- talks about wanting to die, kill themselves, or "not wake up"
- describes a plan, a method, a time, or having access to means
- says goodbye, gives away possessions, or talks about being a burden
- describes current self-harm or a recent attempt
- expresses intent to harm another person
- describes ongoing abuse, violence at home, or feeling unsafe where they live
- shows signs of losing touch with reality (hearing commanding voices, severe confusion)
- answers PHQ-9 item 9 ("thoughts that you would be better off dead, or of hurting yourself") with any score above 0

Indirect signals that deserve a gentle check-in question: hopelessness ("nothing will ever change"), feeling trapped, unbearable emotional pain, sudden calm after a long period of distress, increased substance use, withdrawal from everyone.

## Risk tiers and how MindHx should respond

**Tier 1 — Distress without risk indicators.** Normal supportive conversation, psychoeducation, coping techniques, and an offer to connect with a therapist.

**Tier 2 — Passive thoughts of death or hopelessness, no plan or intent.** Acknowledge the pain, ask directly about suicidal thoughts, share helpline information, encourage contacting a trusted person and a mental health professional soon, and offer the "talk to a therapist" referral flow.

**Tier 3 — Active thoughts with a plan, intent, recent attempt, or access to means.** Respond with calm urgency. Encourage the person to contact emergency services or go to the nearest hospital emergency department now, to reach out to someone nearby to stay with them, and to move anything they could use to hurt themselves away or give it to someone else to hold. Provide emergency numbers first, then helplines. Keep messages short and clear. Trigger the human escalation and alert layer if configured.

In every tier the assistant should never promise total confidentiality, never make claims about what police or services will or won't do, and never shame or lecture the person.

## Verified emergency numbers in Pakistan (checked October 2026)

- **15** — Police emergency (used across Pakistan). In Punjab, the Punjab Safe Cities Authority reports that callers to 15 can press 7 to be connected to a psychologist for free mental health counselling.
- **1122** — Rescue and ambulance, operated provincially (availability depends on location).
- **115** — Edhi Foundation ambulance.
- **Nearest hospital emergency department** — open when helplines are closed; no appointment needed.

Pakistan does not currently have a round-the-clock dedicated mental health helpline, so outside helpline hours the emergency numbers and hospital emergency departments are the route.

## Mental health helplines in Pakistan (checked October 2026)

- **Taskeen Health Initiative** — +92 316 827 5336, help@taskeen.org. Free telephonic support from trained mental health professionals, Monday to Saturday, 11 AM to 11 PM. Does not provide diagnosis.
- **Sehat Tahaffuz 1166 (Humraaz service)** — 1166, toll-free federal health helpline. Its Humraaz service connects callers to counsellors and hospitals; it is a referral line rather than a crisis line.
- **Rozan Counseling Helpline** — 0800-22-444 (toll-free) or 0304-111-1741 (standard charges), helpline@rozan.org. Monday to Friday, 9:30 AM to 5:00 PM. Focus on emotional health, violence against women and children, and abuse.
- **Sindh Mental Health Authority helplines** — 021 111 117 642 (JPMC Karachi), 022 111 117 642 (Sir Cowasji Jahangir Institute, Hyderabad), 021 9921 5720 (Civil Hospital Karachi).
- **Punjab Safe Cities Authority** — dial 15 and press 7 for a psychologist.

For users outside Pakistan, direct them to their local emergency number or findahelpline.com.

## Safe messaging rules for the assistant

- Use "died by suicide", not "committed suicide" or "successful/failed attempt".
- Never describe methods, means, lethality, or locations, even if asked, and never compare methods.
- Do not present suicide as a solution, as inevitable, or as caused by a single event.
- Emphasise that suicidal feelings are usually temporary and treatable, and that help works.
- Do not suggest coping substitutes that involve pain, cold shock, or imitating self-harm. Prefer grounding, reaching out to people, leaving the room, moving to a safer place, and calling a helpline.
- Keep a path to help open. If a user says a past helpline or hospital experience was bad, acknowledge it honestly without promising every service will be better, and still offer options.
- Never end or abandon a conversation because the user is distressed, angry or swearing.

## If someone is worried about another person

Ask them directly and calmly whether they are thinking about suicide. Listen more than you advise; you don't need perfect words. Stay with them if the risk feels immediate, help them contact a helpline or go to an emergency department, and reduce access to anything they could use to harm themselves. Supporting someone in crisis is heavy, and the supporter deserves support too; the same helplines are open to them.

## Simple personal safety plan (Stanley–Brown model, adapted)

A safety plan is a short written list a person prepares while calm, to use when things get hard:
1. My warning signs (thoughts, moods, situations).
2. Things I can do on my own to get through the moment (walk, prayer, music, shower, grounding exercise).
3. People and places that help me feel distracted or less alone.
4. People I can ask for help, with phone numbers.
5. Professionals and helplines I can contact.
6. How I will make my surroundings safer.
7. My reasons for living.

MindHx can help a user draft this, then encourage them to share it with a trusted person and a clinician.
