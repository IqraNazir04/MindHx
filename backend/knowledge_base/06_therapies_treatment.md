## How treatment is usually matched to need (stepped care)

Many guidelines, including NICE, use a stepped-care approach:
1. **Mild or early difficulties:** psychoeducation, guided self-help, digital CBT, exercise, group programmes, problem-solving.
2. **Moderate difficulties:** individual psychological therapy such as CBT, behavioural activation, or IPT.
3. **Severe or complex difficulties:** combination of psychological therapy and medication, under a psychiatrist, sometimes with specialist or inpatient care.
People can move between steps. The best therapy is also one the person can access and feels comfortable with; a good relationship with the therapist predicts outcomes across all therapy types.

## Cognitive Behavioural Therapy (CBT)

CBT is based on the idea that thoughts, feelings, body sensations and behaviours affect each other. Unhelpful thinking patterns and behaviours (like avoidance) keep problems going. CBT is structured, time-limited (often 8–20 sessions), focuses on present problems, and includes practice between sessions. It has the strongest evidence base of any talking therapy for depression, anxiety disorders, panic, social anxiety, OCD (with ERP), PTSD (trauma-focused), insomnia (CBT-I), and chronic pain.

Common CBT tools: thought records, identifying cognitive distortions, behavioural experiments, graded exposure, activity scheduling, and problem-solving.

## Common thinking traps (cognitive distortions)

- **All-or-nothing thinking:** "If it's not perfect, I've failed."
- **Catastrophising:** jumping to the worst outcome.
- **Mind reading:** assuming you know what others think of you.
- **Fortune telling:** predicting things will go badly.
- **Overgeneralising:** "This always happens to me."
- **Should statements:** rigid rules about how you or others must be.
- **Emotional reasoning:** "I feel useless, so I must be useless."
- **Personalising:** blaming yourself for things outside your control.
- **Discounting the positive:** "That success doesn't count."
- **Labelling:** "I'm a failure" instead of "I made a mistake."

A simple thought record: describe the situation, note the emotion and its intensity (0–100), write the automatic thought, list evidence for and against it, write a more balanced thought, re-rate the emotion.

## Behavioural Activation (BA)

Depression pulls people into withdrawal and inactivity, which removes sources of pleasure and achievement and deepens low mood. Behavioural activation reverses this by scheduling small, specific activities that give a sense of enjoyment, achievement, or connection with values, regardless of motivation. Action comes first; motivation follows. BA is as effective as full CBT for depression and is simpler to deliver, making it suitable for trained non-specialists.

How to start: track activities and mood for a few days, list valued activities (easy, medium, hard), schedule one or two easy ones daily, notice mood afterwards, and build up gradually.

## Dialectical Behaviour Therapy (DBT)

DBT, developed by Marsha Linehan, combines CBT with mindfulness and acceptance. It was designed for people with intense emotions, chronic self-harm and suicidal behaviour, and borderline personality disorder. It teaches four skill sets:
- **Mindfulness:** observing and describing without judgement.
- **Distress tolerance:** getting through crises without making things worse (e.g. TIPP, self-soothe with the senses, distraction, radical acceptance).
- **Emotion regulation:** understanding emotions, reducing vulnerability (sleep, food, exercise), and acting opposite to unhelpful urges.
- **Interpersonal effectiveness:** asking for what you need and saying no while keeping relationships and self-respect.
Standard DBT includes individual therapy, a skills group, phone coaching, and a therapist consultation team.

## Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT)

ACT aims to increase psychological flexibility: being present with difficult thoughts and feelings while acting on what matters. Its six processes are acceptance (making room for feelings), cognitive defusion (seeing thoughts as thoughts, e.g. "I'm having the thought that I'm useless"), present-moment awareness, self-as-context (the observing self), values, and committed action. ACT has good evidence for chronic pain, depression, anxiety and stress, and underpins the WHO self-help guide *Doing What Matters in Times of Stress*.

<!-- chunk: id=ther_007 | domain=therapies | type=treatment | risk=low | audience=general | lang=en -->
## Interpersonal Therapy (IPT)

IPT is a time-limited therapy (around 12–16 sessions) that links depression to relationship problems in one of four areas: grief, role disputes (conflicts with important people), role transitions (marriage, becoming a parent, migration, retirement, job loss), and interpersonal deficits or isolation. It improves communication and support. IPT is recommended for depression, including perinatal depression, and group IPT has been effective in low-resource settings.

<!-- chunk: id=ther_008 | domain=therapies | type=treatment | risk=low | audience=general | lang=en -->
## Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy (MBCT) and MBSR

MBCT is an 8-week group programme combining mindfulness meditation with CBT ideas. It teaches people to notice early warning signs of depression and step out of rumination. NICE recommends it for preventing relapse in people with recurrent depression who are currently well. MBSR (Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction) is a similar 8-week programme focused on stress, pain and illness.

## Trauma-focused therapies

Recommended therapies for PTSD (NICE NG116, APA guideline):
- **Trauma-focused CBT**, including **Cognitive Processing Therapy (CPT)**, which addresses stuck beliefs about blame, safety and trust, and **Prolonged Exposure (PE)**, which gradually reduces avoidance of memories and reminders.
- **EMDR (Eye Movement Desensitisation and Reprocessing)**, in which the person briefly focuses on the trauma memory while doing bilateral stimulation (eye movements or taps), helping the memory become less distressing.
- **Narrative Exposure Therapy (NET)**, useful for refugees and people with multiple traumas.
These should be delivered by trained clinicians. Talking through trauma without structure, or being pushed to retell it, can worsen symptoms.

<!-- chunk: id=ther_010 | domain=therapies | type=treatment | risk=low | audience=general | lang=en -->
## Exposure and Response Prevention (ERP)

The first-line therapy for OCD and a core part of treatment for phobias and panic. The person gradually faces feared situations or thoughts (exposure) while resisting compulsions or safety behaviours (response prevention). Anxiety rises then falls, and the brain learns the feared outcome doesn't happen or is bearable. Exposure is planned collaboratively in a hierarchy from easier to harder steps.

<!-- chunk: id=ther_011 | domain=therapies | type=treatment | risk=low | audience=general | lang=en -->
## CBT for Insomnia (CBT-I)

CBT-I usually runs 4–8 sessions and includes: sleep restriction (matching time in bed to actual sleep time, then expanding), stimulus control (bed for sleep only, get up when awake), cognitive work on worries about sleep, relaxation, and sleep hygiene. It is recommended as first-line treatment for chronic insomnia and is effective in digital formats.

## Problem-Solving Therapy and WHO low-intensity programmes

Problem-solving therapy teaches a structured method: define the problem, brainstorm options, weigh pros and cons, choose and plan, act, review. The WHO has developed scalable programmes delivered by trained non-specialists, several tested in Pakistan:
- **Thinking Healthy Programme:** CBT-based, for perinatal depression, delivered by Lady Health Workers.
- **Problem Management Plus (PM+):** five sessions covering stress management, problem-solving, behavioural activation, and strengthening social support; tested in Peshawar.
- **Self-Help Plus (SH+)** and **Doing What Matters in Times of Stress:** ACT-based self-help.
- **Friendship Bench** (Zimbabwe origin): problem-solving therapy by lay health workers, now adapted in several countries.
These support MindHx's model of early help plus referral.

## Other therapies

- **Psychodynamic therapy:** explores how past experiences and unconscious patterns shape current feelings and relationships; short-term versions have evidence for depression.
- **Compassion-Focused Therapy (CFT):** for high shame and self-criticism.
- **Schema Therapy:** for long-standing patterns and personality difficulties.
- **Family and couples therapy:** when relationships are central to the problem, and for adolescents.
- **Group therapy and peer support:** reduce isolation and stigma.
- **Prolonged Grief Disorder Therapy:** targeted grief treatment (see grief file).

## Medication overview (general information only)

Medication is prescribed by a doctor or psychiatrist after assessment. MindHx must not recommend specific drugs, doses, or changes. General information only:
- **SSRIs and SNRIs** (antidepressants) are commonly used for moderate to severe depression and anxiety disorders. They usually take 2–6 weeks to work, early side effects often settle, and stopping suddenly can cause discontinuation symptoms, so changes should be planned with a doctor. Young people under 25 starting antidepressants should be monitored closely for increased suicidal thoughts in the first weeks.
- **Benzodiazepines** may be used short-term for severe anxiety but carry dependence risk and are not recommended long-term.
- **Mood stabilisers and antipsychotics** are used for bipolar disorder and psychosis under specialist care.
- **Sleeping pills** are for short-term use; CBT-I is preferred for chronic insomnia.
Medication and therapy combined are often more effective than either alone for moderate to severe depression. Users with questions about their medication should be directed to their prescriber or pharmacist.

## How to find and choose a therapist

Look for a qualified clinical psychologist, counselling psychologist, or psychiatrist. In Pakistan, check registration with the Pakistan Medical and Dental Council (for psychiatrists) or recognised psychology credentials. Good questions to ask: What approach do you use and for what problems? How many sessions might I need? What does it cost, and is online therapy available? How do you handle confidentiality? It's fine to switch if the fit doesn't feel right after a few sessions. Government hospitals with psychiatry departments often provide lower-cost services.
