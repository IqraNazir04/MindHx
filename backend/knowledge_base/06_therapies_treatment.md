## What therapy is, and isn't

Psychotherapy ("talk therapy") is a structured, collaborative process with a trained professional aimed at understanding and changing patterns of thought, feeling, and behaviour that cause distress. It is not just venting, and it is not advice-giving. Different approaches suit different problems and people; there is no single "best" therapy for everyone. MindHx can explain what therapies exist and help a user think about what might fit, but matching someone to a specific therapist or approach is ultimately a clinical decision made with that professional.

<!-- chunk: id=therapy_002 | domain=therapy | type=treatment | risk=low | audience=general | lang=en -->
## Cognitive Behavioural Therapy (CBT)

CBT is the most researched psychotherapy, with strong evidence for anxiety disorders, depression, OCD, PTSD, insomnia, and chronic pain. It is based on the idea that thoughts, feelings, physical sensations and behaviours are interconnected, and that changing unhelpful thought patterns and behaviours (such as avoidance) can improve how a person feels. Typical CBT involves identifying specific problems, testing and reframing distorted thoughts (catastrophising, all-or-nothing thinking, mind-reading), and behavioural experiments or exposure, usually over 6 to 20 structured sessions with homework between sessions.

## Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT)

ACT focuses on accepting difficult thoughts and feelings rather than fighting them, and committing to actions aligned with personal values even when discomfort is present. Core skills include cognitive defusion (noticing a thought as "just a thought" rather than fact), mindfulness, acceptance, and values clarification. ACT has good evidence for chronic pain, anxiety, depression, and situations where a problem (grief, a medical diagnosis, a difficult circumstance) can't simply be solved away.

<!-- chunk: id=therapy_004 | domain=therapy | type=treatment | risk=moderate | audience=general | lang=en -->
## Dialectical Behaviour Therapy (DBT)

DBT was originally developed for chronic suicidality and Borderline Personality Disorder and is now used more broadly for intense emotion dysregulation, self-harm, and impulsivity. It combines individual therapy, skills groups, and between-session coaching, teaching four skill modules: mindfulness, distress tolerance (getting through crises without making things worse), emotion regulation, and interpersonal effectiveness. DBT has strong evidence for reducing self-harm and hospitalisation in people with significant emotional instability.

## Interpersonal Therapy (IPT) and Psychodynamic Therapy

**IPT** focuses on current relationships and life transitions (grief, role changes, conflict, isolation) and how they connect to mood, with strong evidence for depression, particularly when relationship problems are prominent. **Psychodynamic therapy** explores how past experiences and unconscious patterns shape present relationships and feelings, often over a longer timeframe; brief, time-limited versions have evidence for depression and some personality difficulties.

<!-- chunk: id=therapy_006 | domain=therapy | type=treatment | risk=moderate | audience=general | lang=en -->
## EMDR (Eye Movement Desensitisation and Reprocessing)

EMDR is a structured trauma therapy where a person briefly recalls distressing memories while making guided eye movements or other bilateral stimulation (tapping, sounds), with the aim of reducing the emotional intensity of the memory over repeated sessions. It has strong evidence for PTSD, recommended by NICE and the WHO, and works for some people who find purely verbal trauma processing difficult. It should only be delivered by a trained EMDR clinician.

<!-- chunk: id=therapy_007 | domain=therapy | type=treatment | risk=moderate | audience=general | lang=en -->
## Family and couples therapy

Family therapy treats difficulties in the context of the family system rather than one "identified" individual, and is particularly useful for adolescent mental health, eating disorders, and situations where family conflict or stigma is central to someone's distress. Couples therapy (e.g. Emotionally Focused Therapy) addresses relationship distress, communication breakdown, and attachment injuries. In Pakistani households where family involvement in a person's life is often high, family-inclusive approaches can be more effective than treating the individual in isolation, provided the user consents and feels safe including family.

## Medication: what MindHx should and shouldn't say

Antidepressants (SSRIs, SNRIs), anti-anxiety medication, mood stabilisers, and antipsychotics can be an effective and sometimes necessary part of treatment, often alongside therapy. MindHx must never recommend a specific medication, dose, or brand, must never tell a user to start, stop, increase, decrease, or switch medication, and must never claim a medication is unsafe or ineffective in general terms. Questions about medication should be redirected to the prescribing doctor or a psychiatrist, while validating that it's reasonable to have questions and concerns about medication, including side effects and stigma around taking it.

## How to find a therapist in Pakistan

Options include clinical psychologists, psychiatrists (medical doctors who can prescribe), and licensed counsellors; qualifications and regulation vary, so it is reasonable to ask a provider about their training and experience with a specific issue. University hospitals and teaching hospitals (e.g. Aga Khan University Hospital, Shifa International, Jinnah Postgraduate Medical Centre) often have psychiatry and psychology departments. Several Pakistani telehealth platforms (e.g. Taskeen, Rozan referral network, and various app-based services) also offer online sessions, which can reduce stigma and travel barriers. MindHx can help a user prepare what to say in a first session but should not claim to recommend a specific named clinician it cannot verify.

## Common worries about starting therapy

Normalise common hesitations: fear of being judged, not knowing what to say, worry about cost, family finding out, or past bad experiences with a provider. It's useful to know that a first session is mostly about describing the problem and history, that therapy can take a few sessions to feel like a good fit (and it's fine to try a different therapist if it doesn't), and that confidentiality rules typically protect what's shared except where there is risk to life, which a good therapist will explain upfront.
