MindHx Project Report2

Abstract

MindHx is an early-detection mental-health triage aid designed to lower the barrier to a first, private screening step. Rather than functioning as a diagnostic tool, MindHx fuses three independent signals — acoustic (how a person sounds when speaking), linguistic (what they say, in their own words), and three validated clinical questionnaires (PHQ-9, GAD-7, and K10) — into a single, explainable risk score. The system is built around three non-negotiable design commitments: it must be multimodal rather than relying on a single signal; it must route elevated risk to a human professional rather than attempting to diagnose; and it must collect the minimum possible data, with accounts entirely optional and raw audio and text never persisted.

A central and deliberate focus of this project is full bilingual support for English and Urdu. Every page of the application — the check-in flow, the AI chat, the therapist directory, the meditation and therapy reference libraries — exists in both languages, switchable with a single tap, with the layout correctly mirroring for Urdu's right-to-left script. This is treated as a first-class architectural requirement, not a translation layer added after the fact, because a screening tool available only in English quietly excludes a large portion of the population it is intended to serve, particularly in Pakistan.

The application also includes a bounded, source-grounded AI chatbot intended for everyday stress, anxiety, and burnout — situations that rarely reach a mental-health professional but still affect daily functioning. The chatbot answers exclusively from a fixed, reviewed reference library, never improvising medical advice, and is protected by multiple layers of safety logic: a crisis-language gate that runs before any content is generated, and a server-side script-detection guard that discards any AI-generated Urdu reply that is not actually written in Urdu.

This report documents the complete system: its motivation and objectives, its architecture and technology stack, the implementation of each major subsystem, the testing performed to validate correctness and safety, and an honest account of the project's current limitations and the future work required to move from a bilingual demonstration to a clinically validated, production-grade screening tool.

01  Introduction

1.1 Background and Motivation

Depression, anxiety, and related mental-health conditions are common but frequently under-screened. Two forces drive this gap. The first is structural: access to psychiatrists and psychologists is limited, especially outside major urban centers, and a first appointment often carries real cost in money, time, and travel. The second is social: seeking mental-health support is, in many communities, still read as a sign of weakness or a source of family shame rather than an ordinary health matter — a dynamic that is especially pronounced in South Asian contexts, including Pakistan.

MindHx was conceived to address the specific moment before either of those barriers is engaged: the moment a person first notices that something feels off, and would rather understand it a little before deciding what, if anything, to do next. That moment does not require a clinic, an appointment, or a disclosure to anyone — it requires a private, low-friction way to notice a pattern.

1.2 Problem Statement

Existing digital mental-health tools tend to fall into one of two categories, each with a significant gap. Simple self-assessment apps ask a single type of question (typically a mood questionnaire) and return a score with no explanation of how it was derived, no connection between the score and what a person should do next, and often no consideration of language accessibility. On the other end, AI chatbot products designed for emotional support frequently generate free-form advice with no hard boundary against improvised medical claims, and no reliable mechanism to detect and escalate a genuine safety crisis before continuing the conversation.

Furthermore, almost all such tools are built and shipped in English only. For a country such as Pakistan, where Urdu is the national language and lingua franca, an English-only screening tool systematically excludes a large share of the population most likely to benefit from it.

1.3 Objectives

  1. Design and implement a multimodal check-in that combines acoustic, linguistic, and validated clinical-questionnaire signals into one explainable, weighted risk score.
  2. Ensure the system routes — rather than diagnoses — by using risk-tier language throughout and by short-circuiting to emergency-support routing the instant a crisis signal is detected, before any score is computed or shown.
  3. Build the entire application bilingually in English and Urdu from the architectural ground up, including a fully mirrored right-to-left layout for Urdu.
  4. Provide a bounded, source-grounded AI chatbot for everyday stress and anxiety that cannot improvise medical advice and defers immediately on any safety concern.
  5. Minimize data collection by design: no account required for the core flow, no persisted audio or transcripts, and optional accounts that store only aggregate results.
  6. Connect a person to real, verified next steps — a Pakistan-specific therapist and psychiatrist directory, and practical meditation/therapy reference material — rather than leaving the screening result as a dead end.

1.4 Scope

MindHx is explicitly scoped as a screening and triage aid, not a diagnostic or treatment system. It does not replace a licensed clinician, does not provide crisis counseling, and does not prescribe or recommend medication. Its combined risk score is a weighted heuristic rather than a clinically calibrated probability, and its Urdu translation of the PHQ-9, GAD-7, and K10 questionnaires is a careful draft for demonstration purposes rather than a licensed clinical instrument — both facts are disclosed openly within the application itself and within this report, rather than hidden or overstated.

02  Importance for Pakistan

While MindHx is designed to be useful anywhere, its architecture and content were deliberately shaped around the realities of mental-health access in Pakistan, for four concrete reasons.

2.1 A Structural Access Gap

Pakistan has one of the lowest psychiatrist-to-population ratios in the world; widely cited estimates put the number of practicing psychiatrists nationally under 500 for a population exceeding 240 million, with those professionals concentrated in a small number of major cities. For a large share of the population — particularly outside Karachi, Lahore, and Islamabad — a first psychiatric consultation is simply not a short-notice option.

2.2 Social Stigma

Seeking mental-health support is frequently perceived as a sign of personal weakness or a source of family shame rather than an ordinary health matter. This social cost discourages people from taking even the first, private step of understanding what they are experiencing, independent of whether professional care is ultimately affordable or accessible.

2.3 The Language Gap

Urdu is Pakistan's national language and its most widely understood lingua franca, yet the overwhelming majority of digital mental-health tools — screening instruments, chatbots, and educational content alike — are available only in English. An English-only tool does not merely inconvenience Urdu-speaking users; it silently excludes precisely the population segment least likely to already have access to English-language mental-health resources through other channels.

2.4 Cost and Distance

Even where a psychiatrist or psychologist is geographically reachable, a first appointment carries a real cost in consultation fees, transportation, and time away from work or family responsibilities — a cost many people are unwilling to bear simply to find out whether their situation warrants further attention.

MindHx's response. The application is built directly against all four barriers: it is private and free to try, requires no account for its core functionality, is fully bilingual with a properly mirrored Urdu experience, and is paired with a real, verified, city-by-city directory of psychiatric and psychological care providers in Pakistan — turning "I should probably see someone" into an actual, concrete next step.

2.5 Everyday Mental Health, Not Just Crisis Mental Health

Most people in Pakistan never consult a professional for the kind of mental-health friction that affects daily life without rising to crisis level: poor sleep, work pressure, household responsibility overload, or ordinary daily anxiety. MindHx's AI chatbot is explicitly designed to serve this middle ground — offering simple, safe, immediately actionable meditation techniques and basic therapy-approach education without requiring an appointment or a wait, while still recognizing and redirecting the small number of conversations that turn serious toward real emergency support instead of continuing unsupervised.

04  System Architecture and Design

4.1 High-Level Architecture

MindHx is built as two independently deployable services: a Next.js 16 frontend (App Router) and a FastAPI backend. The screening and risk-assessment endpoints are fully stateless — every request is self-contained and nothing is written to a database unless a user has explicitly created an optional account. 4.2 Multimodal Signal Fusion

The core of MindHx's screening logic is the fusion of three independent signals into one combined, explainable risk score. Each signal is scored on its own scale first, then combined using an additive weighted model. Because the model is additive, each term's contribution to the final score is the exact — not approximated — Shapley-equivalent attribution, which is what allows the results page to show a genuine per-signal breakdown rather than a black-box number.

4.4 Bilingual Architecture and the Urdu Safety Guard

Bilingual support is implemented at the data level, not as a runtime translation service. Every page defines one bilingual copy object keyed by language, and a single header-level toggle switches both the active language and the text direction (dir="rtl") together, so CSS flexbox and logical properties correctly mirror the entire layout rather than only swapping words inside a left-to-right frame.

The AI chat introduces an additional risk: an LLM (Qwen) is instructed via its system prompt to reply in the requested language, but large language models do not always comply with such instructions. 06  Implementation Details

6.1 Frontend

The frontend is organized as one route per major feature: the home check-in, results, medication reference, AI chat, meditation and therapy libraries (each with dynamic per-item detail pages), the therapist directory, emergency support, a brand/design-system page, and the optional login/register/dashboard flow. Every top-level route follows a consistent pattern — a thin server component (page.tsx) exports Next.js Metadata (title, description, canonical URL, Open Graph tags) and renders an accompanying client component that holds the actual interactive logic. This split exists specifically because Next.js cannot export page metadata from a client component, and per-page search-relevant titles were a deliberate requirement rather than an afterthought.

Shared components include SiteHeader and SiteFooter (sticky navigation and a dark full-width footer present on every page), an original hand-built Doodles illustration set, a NatureBanner component wrapping verified free-to-use photography with visible photographer attribution, an original glossy-3D StepIcons/SignalGraphics icon system replacing generic emoji and line icons across the product, and a ProtectedRoute client-side auth gate for the dashboard.

6.2 Backend and API

The FastAPI backend exposes 20 REST endpoints spanning five concerns: transcription and acoustic/text analysis, questionnaire scoring and fused risk assessment, the bounded AI chat, optional authentication, and check-in/mood/practice persistence for signed-in users. Every screening-relevant endpoint is stateless by default; the only endpoints that touch a database are the authentication and history endpoints, and only when a user has explicitly created an account.

POST /risk-assess # fuses voice + text + PHQ-9/GAD-7/K10 into one explainable score
POST /ai/chat # safety-gated, source-grounded chat reply
POST /auth/register, /auth/login # optional account creation/sign-in
GET  /auth/me # returns the current user for a valid Bearer token

6.3 The RAG Chatbot

The AI chat is a retrieval-augmented, bounded system rather than an open-ended conversational agent. A small library of reviewed reference documents (RAG_DOCUMENTS) covers common early-stage themes — anxiety, depression, therapy, medication, family stigma, and exam/work pressure — each with a matched English and Urdu version. An incoming message is matched to the relevant document(s) by keyword, and if an LLM provider is configured, Qwen composes a natural-language reply constrained to cite only that retrieved material, returning strict JSON validated against an allow-list of interactive-exercise IDs and CTA links before anything is shown to the user. If no LLM key is configured, or the LLM's output fails validation (including the crisis-language and Urdu-script checks described in Sections 4.3–4.4), the system falls back to the deterministic reference-document reply — the chat is never left unanswered, it simply loses the conversational layer.

6.4 Urdu Language Support

Urdu support spans five layers: (1) a bilingual copy object on every page; (2) a header-level toggle that switches language and text direction together; (3) a dedicated Nastaliq-appropriate heading font and RTL-specific typography rules (line-height and font-size), added after an early implementation defect caused Urdu headings to visually overlap when reusing Latin display-font metrics; (4) fully translated RAG reference content and interactive-exercise instructions; and (5) the server-side is_urdu_script() defense-in-depth guard described in Section 4.4.

6.5 Explainable Risk Scoring

The combined score uses an additive weighted model, meaning the exact contribution of each signal to the final number is available in closed form rather than requiring an approximation technique such as sampled Shapley values. This was a deliberate modeling choice: it trades some of the expressive power of a non-linear model for the ability to show a person, truthfully, "here is exactly how much your voice, your words, and your questionnaire answers each moved this number."

6.6 Original Visual Design System

Rather than sourcing third-party icon packs or stock illustration, MindHx uses two original, hand-authored SVG systems built specifically for this project:

  • StepIcons — a library of roughly 40 glossy, 3D-shaded badge icons (radial-gradient body, inset highlight, drop shadow) illustrating individual steps within each meditation technique and therapy approach, replacing plain numbered lists.
  • SignalGraphics and MoodFace — large (96px) illustrated graphics for the home page's three signal cards (a microphone with sound-wave arcs, a chat bubble, a clinical clipboard with a heartbeat pulse), and a five-expression glossy mood-face set replacing operating-system emoji in the daily mood check-in widget.

All photography used across the product (nature banners on every resource page) is sourced from Unsplash and individually verified to exclude Unsplash+ premium images, with photographer name and profile linked in a visible attribution line on every banner, per the Unsplash license's attribution guidance.

6.7 Search Engine Optimization

Because every page was originally a client component, the entire site shared one generic browser-tab title. This was corrected by splitting every route into a thin server-rendered wrapper carrying distinct, search-relevant metadata, plus a sitemap.xml covering every static and dynamic route, a robots.txt excluding private/authenticated pages from indexing, a dynamically generated favicon matching the in-app brand mark, and WebApplication JSON-LD structured data on the home page.

07  Core Functionality Walkthrough

7.1 The Check-In

A person opens the home page and is presented with three signal cards: How you sound (an optional 60-second voice recording), What you say (a free-text box, analyzed for sentiment and never stored), and Clinical check-in (PHQ-9, GAD-7, and K10 administered in sequence, in the selected language). A live, client-side estimate updates as each signal is completed, before the user ever submits anything to the server.

7.2 Results and Explanation

Submitting the check-in calls /risk-assess, which returns a combined score, a risk band, a routing decision, detected themes, and — critically — a per-signal attribution breakdown rendered directly on the results page, alongside a support plan matched to the detected themes (grounding techniques for anxiety, behavioral-activation prompts for low motivation, pacing guidance after loss or trauma, and so on).

7.3 The AI Chat

A person can continue the conversation in the MindHx AI chat, which grounds every reply in the reviewed reference library described in Section 6.3, offers interactive exercises (4-7-8 breathing, 5-4-3-2-1 grounding) directly inline, and — for signed-in users — quietly incorporates recent mood trend and previously-helpful practices into its tone without ever reciting the underlying numbers back to the user.

7.4 Resource Libraries

The meditation library documents six grounding and relaxation techniques (box breathing, 5-4-3-2-1 grounding, body scan, one small action, progressive muscle relaxation, mindful walking); the therapies library documents six evidence-based approaches (CBT, DBT, exposure therapy, trauma-informed therapy, medical review, and peer support groups). Every technique and therapy detail page illustrates its steps with the original 3D icon system described in Section 6.6.

7.5 Finding a Professional

The therapist page frames what to say at a first appointment and what to bring, then offers a city-searchable directory combining curated, verified real institutions (for example, in Karachi: Karwan-e-Hayat Institute for Mental Health Care, Civil Hospital Karachi's Department of Psychiatry, and Karachi Psychiatric Hospital) with live links to established individual-provider directories (oladoc.com, Marham.pk) that stay current without MindHx needing to maintain a static, decaying list itself.

7.6 Emergency Support

Reachable from the top navigation on every page at all times, the Emergency Support page provides immediate-action guidance framed explicitly as a risk signal rather than a diagnosis, and is the automatic destination the moment the safety gate (Section 4.3) is triggered.

08  Testing and Quality Assurance

Correctness and, especially, safety-critical behavior are verified with an automated backend test suite (pytest, 23 tests) rather than manual inspection alone. Test coverage includes:

  • Crisis short-circuiting via PHQ-9 item 9 and via free-text self-harm language, including a regression test confirming this cannot be bypassed even when conversation history, screening context, and mood-trend data are supplied alongside the crisis message.
  • Theme detection accuracy (anxiety, depression, therapy, medication, family stigma, exam/work pressure), including a regression test for a previously fixed keyword-matching defect.
  • Bilingual AI chat responses, including a dedicated test asserting that Urdu-requested replies contain predominantly non-ASCII (Urdu-script) characters, and a test that a fabricated English LLM reply is correctly discarded when Urdu was requested (Section 4.4).
  • The prosodic acoustic-signal math, tested independently of whether the optional PyAV dependency is available in the runtime environment.
  • The full account lifecycle: register, login, /auth/me, saved check-ins, mood check-ins, and helpful-practice recording.
  • The risk-assessment fusion shape and score boundaries for PHQ-9 (0–27), GAD-7 (0–21), and K10 (10–50).

Beyond automated tests, the frontend is verified with TypeScript's strict compiler (tsc --noEmit) and ESLint on every change, and every significant visual or bilingual change in this project was verified with real headless-browser screenshots — in both English and Urdu — rather than assumed correct from the source code, a discipline that directly surfaced and led to the fix of several real defects, including a flexbox spacing bug in the site logo, an animated graphic overlapping hero text, and the Urdu heading line-height/overlap defect described in Section 6.4.

09  Results and Discussion

The completed system delivers a fully functional, end-to-end triage experience across 12 frontend routes and 20 backend endpoints, with complete feature and language parity between English and Urdu. The explainable-attribution design goal was achieved exactly as specified: because the fusion model is additive, the results page can show a mathematically exact — not approximated — breakdown of each signal's contribution, which is a meaningfully stronger explainability guarantee than the "feature importance" outputs typical of black-box models.

The safety-gate design goal was validated both by direct testing (Section 8) and by construction: crisis detection runs as an independent check ahead of, not inside, the scoring and chat-generation pipelines, so it cannot be quietly weakened by a future change to either of those pipelines without also changing the gate itself.

The bilingual goal proved to be the most implementation-intensive commitment in the project. Achieving genuine parity required more than translated strings: it required RTL-aware CSS throughout, a script-appropriate heading font and metrics (Section 6.4), and — for the AI chat specifically — a second, independent safety mechanism to guard against an LLM silently reverting to English. The project's experience suggests that "bilingual support" for a right-to-left script is best treated as a distinct architectural requirement from the outset, rather than a checklist item added after a left-to-right design is otherwise complete; several of the defects found during this project (heading overlap, an untranslated nature-banner caption on two pages) trace directly to places where that discipline slipped.

The privacy-by-design goal (no persisted audio, no persisted transcript, optional-only accounts) was maintained throughout without exception, including under feature pressure — for example, an Alibaba Cloud OSS-based audio-caching feature was considered and explicitly declined during development because it would have contradicted the project's "nothing is saved by default" commitment.

10  Limitations

The following limitations are disclosed deliberately, both within the application itself and here, because a screening tool that overstates its own certainty is more dangerous than one that plainly states what it does not yet know.

  • The combined risk score is not clinically calibrated. It is a weighted heuristic that has not been fit or validated against real diagnostic-outcome data, and should be read as a relative risk-tier indicator rather than a calibrated probability.
  • The Urdu translation of PHQ-9, GAD-7, and K10 is not a licensed clinical instrument. It is a careful draft translation appropriate for demonstration purposes; item 9 (self-harm ideation) is safety-critical in the validated original, and shipping an unlicensed translation as clinically equivalent would be irresponsible.
  • The acoustic voice signal is a local heuristic, not a validated biomarker. Pause ratio, loudness variability, and speaking rate are explicit proxies, intentionally isolated behind a pluggable provider interface so a real, validated vendor could be integrated later without changing the rest of the pipeline.
  • Authentication has not yet been hardened for production scale. The current implementation covers password hashing and token expiry but lacks login rate-limiting/brute-force protection, and stores the access token in localStorage rather than an httpOnly cookie for implementation simplicity — a real concern given the sensitivity of the data an account can be linked to.
  • The therapist directory is curated, not exhaustive. It combines a small number of manually verified institutions with live third-party directory links, and is explicitly framed as a starting point rather than an endorsement.

11  Future Work

11.1 Score Calibration

Once real, labeled outcome data becomes available (for example, through a partnership with a clinical institution willing to compare MindHx's signal against eventual clinical assessment), techniques such as Platt scaling or isotonic regression could convert the current relative risk-tier score into an actual calibrated probability — a prerequisite for any future clinical-deployment claim.

11.2 A Validated Urdu Clinical Instrument

Partnering with a qualified translator and a clinical reviewer to produce and validate a proper Urdu PHQ-9/GAD-7/K10 translation is identified as the single highest-leverage remaining localization gap — it would allow MindHx to move from a bilingual demonstration to a genuine screening tool usable in Urdu-speaking clinical settings.

11.3 A Real Acoustic Biomarker Vendor

The existing VOICE_BIOMARKER_PROVIDER abstraction was built specifically so a clinically validated speech-biomarker vendor with a public API contract could be integrated as a drop-in replacement for today's local heuristic, likely the single largest lever on overall score reliability given that the text and questionnaire signals are already backed by either a real classifier model or validated instruments.

11.4 Deeper Alibaba Cloud Integration

Qwen via DashScope is already integrated as the preferred text-analysis and chat-generation provider; further Alibaba-native deployment — for example, Function Compute for the FastAPI service — is a natural next step for any deployment targeting that cloud stack, while continuing to avoid persistent object storage for user audio, which would contradict the project's core privacy commitment.

11.5 Additional Regional Languages

Because the bilingual architecture (Section 4.4) is built around a per-page copy object and a direction-aware layout rather than any Urdu-specific assumption, the same pattern generalizes to other regional languages such as Punjabi, Pashto, or Sindhi with comparatively modest additional engineering effort.

11.6 Authentication Hardening

Adding login rate-limiting and migrating the access token from localStorage to an httpOnly cookie are both identified as necessary steps before the optional-accounts feature should be considered ready for real users at scale.

12  Conclusion

MindHx demonstrates that a mental-health screening tool can be simultaneously private, explainable, safety-first, and genuinely bilingual without treating any of those four properties as optional or as an afterthought bolted onto a simpler design. By fusing three independent, honestly-scoped signals into one transparent score, by routing rather than diagnosing, by defaulting to storing nothing, and by building the Urdu experience as a first-class architectural requirement rather than a translation pass, the project delivers a tool that meaningfully lowers the barrier to a first mental-health screening step — particularly for a Pakistani audience facing real, structural, and social barriers to that first step today.

Equally important to what has been built is what has been deliberately left unbuilt or clearly labeled as provisional: an uncalibrated score, a demo-grade Urdu clinical translation, and a heuristic rather than validated acoustic signal. This project treats honesty about those limitations as inseparable from the product's core safety commitment, on the view that a screening tool which oversells its own certainty poses a greater risk than one that plainly discloses what it does not yet know. The roadmap in Section 11 charts a concrete path from the current bilingual demonstration toward a properly validated, production-grade screening platform.
